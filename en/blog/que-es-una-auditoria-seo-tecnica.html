What Is a Technical SEO Audit (and Why Most Fall Short)

When someone asks for "an SEO audit," they often get a report auto-generated by a free tool: a list of 40 generic alerts, unprioritized, with no business context, and with no real cross-referenced data source. That's not an audit — it's a scan. A real technical SEO audit starts with data, not a checklist.

The difference between "reviewing" and "auditing"

Reviewing a site means looking at it and giving your opinion on what looks wrong. Auditing it means cross-referencing evidence from multiple sources until the data — not intuition — tells you where the problem really is. For example: a page can "look" well optimized and still not bring in a single real click — you only discover that by checking real performance in Search Console, not by looking at the source code.

The 4F Method: how I audit

I use my own methodology I call the 4F Method — cross-referencing 4 independent data sources before recommending any change:

  • Google Search Console: which keywords are already generating real impressions or clicks, and the indexing status of the entire domain (crawled, not indexed, redirected, etc.).
  • Google Analytics 4: which pages receive real organic traffic, and how engaged those users are (time on page, engagement rate) compared to other channels.
  • Semrush (or another ranking tool): real search volume for target keywords, domain visibility versus competitors, and the site's overall technical health.
  • The site's source code and CMS: a direct inspection of metadata, schema markup, headings, and internal linking — the kind of thing no automated tool checks with real context.

When these 4 sources agree on the same problem, you know it's real and worth prioritizing. When only one source flags it, it's probably noise.

Why this matters more than "fixing everything"

A large site can have hundreds of minor technical "problems" that, in practice, don't move the needle at all. The real value of an audit isn't finding the most errors — it's telling you which 3 or 4 things, if you fix them first, will move the most traffic or conversions. That's why every audit I deliver comes with a plan organized into three horizons: quick wins (0–30 days), consolidation (30–60 days), and ongoing monitoring — not a flat, unordered task list.

A common red flag: pages crawled but not indexed

One of the most frequent findings I run into with the 4F Method is a high volume of pages Google crawls but decides not to index. This eats up crawl budget that takes priority away from new content, and it's invisible if you only look at the site "from the outside" — it only shows up by cross-referencing the Search Console coverage report against the site's actual structure. It's exactly the kind of finding a surface-level review never catches.
